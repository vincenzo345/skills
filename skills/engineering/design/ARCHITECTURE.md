# Architecture design

Design system responsibilities, dependencies, data ownership, and operation around concrete constraints. Inspect the existing system before proposing a replacement. An architecture question can be small; cover only the consequences that matter to it.

1. **Ground the constraints.** Trace the relevant request or job through current code, data stores, external dependencies, and deployment. Read existing decisions and operational evidence. Establish required behavior and material constraints such as load, latency, consistency, availability, security, cost, team capacity, and migration obligations. Label unmeasured quantities as unknown; ask the human to prioritize conflicting goals rather than inventing targets.
2. **Compare viable structures.** Include retaining or incrementally changing the current structure when viable. Show a responsibility and dependency diagram plus representative data and control flows. Explain what each alternative owns, where state lives, how failures propagate, and what becomes easier or harder to change. Compare alternatives against the established constraints, including operational and migration cost.
3. **Resolve consequential tradeoffs.** Recommend a direction with evidence. Involve the human where choices change product behavior, operational commitments, cost, or team responsibilities. Let established standards settle routine details. Load the codebase branch only when a module's interface or seam needs design; use its canonical vocabulary for that question.
4. **Exercise the choice.** Trace a normal operation, a relevant failure or recovery case, and a likely future change through the proposed structure. Check rollout, compatibility, observability, and rollback where existing users or data are affected. Use a bounded prototype or technical experiment for the risky unknown; specify what observation would support or reject the choice.

Deliver the chosen structure, evidence and tradeoffs, ownership and dependency rules, significant interface contracts, migration implications, and open risks. Link the relevant existing decisions and record an ADR when appropriate. A diagram is sufficient only when it explains the important behavior and consequences of the decision.
